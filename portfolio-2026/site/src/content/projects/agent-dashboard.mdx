<Section id="context" number="01" label="Context">
## The day is a queue. The dashboard was a filing cabinet.

Jabu’s agents spend their day on calls: a customer orders, a callback is due, a follow-up is overdue, a basket needs a nudge. The dashboard is the surface between the agent and that queue. If it hides the next call, the agent pays for it on every conversation.

The old board tried to be comprehensive. Customer information was fixed at the top left and took up space that belonged to the work. Orders, GMV, basket size, SKUs, last order, last interaction and promotional offers were all present at once. The result was a long scroll and a hunt for the section that mattered.

The cost was not aesthetic. It was operational: more time per call, missed context, and performance data that lived somewhere else when the agent needed it. Agents were also working without a filter for customer group or type, which made a large book of business feel larger than it was.

The brief was a redesign. The real task was to decide what the board was for.
</Section>

<Section id="research" number="02" label="Research">
## Watching the work before changing the screen

I combined behavioural data with conversation. Hotjar showed what agents actually used and what they ignored. The agent conversations explained why.

Six findings came out of the research:

- Agents needed a **filtering function** for specific groups or types of customer.
- Too much initial information caused a **long scroll** and made sections hard to locate.
- Agents wanted a **separate performance view**, not a panel squeezed into the board.
- A **call-result logic tree** would improve the data captured for retention.
- The **fixed customer-information frame** consumed space that the call needed.
- Agents wanted **customer offers in the dashboard**, rather than switching to the mobile app.

Then we tested the direction with **five agents in Namibia and Zambia**, remote, 15–20 minutes each. The prototype was in Figma, sessions were recorded, and notes lived in Notion.

**75% completed the assigned task independently.** The positives were specific: the board was easier to navigate, the call log was convenient, and one participant described “a wealth of information” reachable in one or two clicks.

The problems were equally specific. Most users struggled to find the daily-target drawer. Some did not understand the campaign wording. And agents wanted a way to mark a customer as removed, non-existent or duplicate so the person stopped appearing in the call list.

<Quote cite="Usability test finding">
The board made the information easier to reach. It did not yet make the next action obvious enough.
</Quote>
</Section>

<Section id="bet" number="03" label="The bet">
## Rebuild the information architecture before the interface

The hypothesis was simple: if the board is organised around the call, agents will spend less time locating information and more time in the conversation.

That meant three moves:

1. **Categorise the information** into Stats, Call-related and General info.
2. **Give the call a stable frame** with the customer context, the call result and the next action in one place.
3. **Move performance out of the board** into its own view, so the working surface stays focused.

Success would be measured through task completion, time on task and the number of misroutes or missed follow-ups. In the first round, we could measure completion and comprehension. The live metrics would come after release.

**In scope:** information architecture, dashboard UI, call states, filtering, customer tags, the performance view and the component library.

**Out of scope:** a full CRM rebuild, a mobile agent app, and a reporting suite. The dashboard had to ship inside the existing product.
</Section>

<Section id="design" number="05" label="Design">
## From an inventory to three zones

The design work started with an inventory, not a layout. We listed every data point on the old board, then asked two questions of each: does the agent need this to make the call, and does it need to be visible before the call starts?

That produced the three-zone structure: **Stats** for the commercial picture, **Call-related** for the interaction and its outcome, and **General info** for the customer’s history and recommendations. The old-to-new comparison became the argument for the redesign.

<Figure number="01" caption="The information architecture: from a dense board, through an audit of every data point, to three zones with a clear job.">
  <Diagram variant="ia" />
</Figure>

The final board is deliberately non-scrollable in its working state. Each zone has a fixed role, and the visual hierarchy does the navigation. The call-outcome section stays hidden until a call is active, which reduces the cognitive load before the conversation and makes the result step explicit once it begins.

Customer tags — New, Retention, Lost, Recover, Current — turn purchase behaviour into a scanning aid. Colour blocking separates the zones without turning the board into a dashboard of competing widgets. The daily-target drawer gives the agent a reason to look up without pulling them out of the queue.

The states were part of the design, not an afterthought: an empty queue, an incoming call, a callback that is due, a follow-up with no answer, and a customer who should be removed from the list.

<Section id="system" number="06" label="System">
## A small library with a long life

The dashboard needed a library because the product was growing faster than its visual language. I defined colour tokens, a type scale and a component set that could survive the next few screens.

The library covered:

- **Colour:** Lucky blue, Jabu purple, neutrals, success, error and progress.
- **Type:** a compact scale for dense operational data.
- **Components:** buttons, checkboxes, dropdowns, text fields, tags and status patterns.
- **Rules:** density limits, alignment, where colour is allowed to carry meaning, and how a state is announced.

The point was not consistency for its own sake. In an operational tool, a repeated pattern is a learned pattern. If a status chip looks different on every screen, the agent has to re-read it. If it looks the same, they can scan it.

<Section id="build" number="07" label="Build">
## Shipping the quieter board

I worked with product and engineering through the build, reviewing the board against the IA rather than against the mockup. The most useful questions were structural: does the call panel still hold when the customer has no order history? Does the empty queue explain what to do next? Does a filter that returns nothing offer a way back?

Design QA focused on the moments the research had surfaced: the daily-target drawer, the campaign copy, and the path for marking a customer as removed or duplicate. Not all of those were solved in the first release, but naming them made them part of the roadmap instead of a surprise.

The board shipped. The library outlived it.
</Section>

## What the test showed

Five agents completed the assigned task, with 75% doing so independently. That is a task-completion signal from a small sample, not a speed claim and not a live product metric.

The qualitative result mattered too. Agents could describe where things were. The call log was seen as convenient. The board felt like a place to work rather than a record to search.

<Callout label="The caveat" tone="cobalt">
This was a five-agent usability test across two markets, not a controlled study. It tells us the structure was learnable. It does not tell us the board reduced handle time in production. The next measurement should be time on task and follow-up completion after release.
</Callout>

The redesign also contributed the component patterns that became part of Jabu’s shared design library. The work did not end at the dashboard; it changed the vocabulary for the next operational screen.
</Section>

<Section id="reflection" number="09" label="Reflection">
## What I would do differently

I would test the daily-target drawer as its own task, not as a detail inside a larger one. It was the most consistent stumble in the sessions, and we learned about it later than we needed to.

I would also instrument the live board earlier. The usability test told us the structure was understandable; it could not tell us whether the queue actually moved faster. A small set of events — time to first call, call-result completion, follow-up created — would have turned a good redesign into a measurable one.

What I would keep is the discipline of the inventory. Listing every data point and asking what job it does is unglamorous, but it is the difference between a tidier screen and a better operating tool.

**The lesson:** in operational tools, hierarchy is the feature. The agent does not need fewer capabilities; they need the next action to be obvious.
</Section>

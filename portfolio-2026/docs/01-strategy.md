# 01 — Strategy

## The 2026 context

The bar for a product design portfolio has moved in five ways:

1. **Judgment is the differentiator.** Everyone can produce polished screens. Hiring managers want to see how you frame ambiguity, choose a direction, and defend a trade-off.
2. **Evidence beats process theater.** A journey map with no decision attached is decoration. A metric, a shipped screen, or a changed behavior is evidence.
3. **AI fluency is assumed.** Not "I use Figma AI," but how AI changed your research, prototyping, or product thinking — and where human judgment stayed.
4. **Systems thinking is expected.** Components, states, edge cases, governance, adoption. Craft is table stakes; scale is the signal.
5. **The site itself is a work sample.** Performance, accessibility, responsiveness, and detail are evaluated as design decisions.

The new portfolio should be built for these five expectations, not for a visual trend.

## Positioning

### The recommendation

**Primary:** Senior product designer for complex B2B products — fintech, loyalty, and CRM.
**Secondary:** Founding / 0→1 designer for small teams.
**Level:** **Senior — confirmed.** The work has to demonstrate senior scope: platform-level systems, research that changed direction, influence beyond execution, and outcomes. Craft alone will not carry the claim.

### Why this positioning

- It is true to the actual work: Perx (loyalty platform), Jabu (fintech wallet, agent CRM), Mentorskool (B2B learning platform).
- The 2026 engagement-archetype project is platform-level work: one configurable system serving telecom, banking, retail, and travel, rather than a single screen.
- It is specific enough to be memorable and broad enough to support multiple roles.
- It turns "sole designer" into a strength, framed correctly: ownership, range, and business exposure — plus the judgment to know what not to build.
- It makes the finance background an asset, not a detour: business literacy, trust, and money products.
- It creates a natural visual metaphor: complex systems have layers; good design goes below the surface.

### Positioning statement

> For product teams building complex B2B products, Monalisha is a **senior product designer** who takes ambiguous problems from research to shipped systems. She brings finance literacy, end-to-end ownership, and a systems mindset — and she stays until the build is right.

### What we are not positioning as

- A visual/brand designer.
- A pure UX researcher.
- A "unicorn" who does everything at surface level.
- A generic "passionate product designer."
- A junior generalist with four years of scattered projects.

## Narrative

### The story

Most of Monalisha's work has been in small teams where the designer owns the whole problem: the research, the flows, the interface, the system, and the QA pass that keeps it honest. She started in finance, moved into product design, and has spent the last four years on products where trust, retention, and operational complexity matter more than visual novelty.

### The arc

1. **Finance gave her the business lens.** She understands how money, risk, and operations actually work.
2. **Small teams gave her range.** Research, flows, UI, systems, handoff, QA — not a slice.
3. **Complex products gave her judgment.** Fintech trust, loyalty mechanics, CRM queues, multi-sided platforms.
4. **Platform work gave her scale.** A configurable engagement system for four industries, and a wallet that has to work for merchants, agents, and Jabu's own collections. The next step is bigger stakes, not a bigger job title.

### The promise

**Design that holds up when you look closer.**

This is the core idea: the work is not just the surface screen. It is the research underneath, the structure, the system, the edge cases, and the shipped outcome. The 3D layer metaphor makes this promise literal.

## Differentiation

### 1. Finance-to-design background

Most product designers cannot read a P&L, a risk model, or an operations queue. Monalisha can. This is a real advantage for fintech, commerce, and B2B workflows — and it is a memorable story.

**Proof:** the engagement archetype case study (one system, four industries), the wallet case study (trust, cash-first behavior), the CRM case study (operational queues), the finance chapter in the timeline.

### 2. End-to-end ownership in small teams

She has been the only designer in the room, which means she has made decisions across research, IA, UI, systems, and QA. The story should frame this as ownership and judgment, not as a lack of collaboration.

**Proof:** scope, decisions, handoff, design QA, peer critique, component library.

### 3. Systems plus craft

She can design the screen and the system behind it: components, states, tokens, documentation, FlutterFlow library, design QA.

**Proof:** `/systems` page, CRM design system, component counts, adoption, ship speed.

### 4. Business literacy

She understands the commercial problem before the interface: activation, retention, support load, trust, conversion.

**Proof:** outcome metrics, business framing in case studies, finance background.

### 5. 2026 fluency

AI-assisted research or prototyping, with clear guardrails; not tool-name dropping.

**Proof:** a dedicated section on `/systems` showing the workflow, artifacts, and what changed.

## Voice and tone

### Principles

- **Precise.** Concrete nouns, real numbers, specific decisions.
- **Warm.** First person, human asides, no corporate gloss.
- **Confident, not arrogant.** State what you did; acknowledge the team and the constraints.
- **Short.** One idea per paragraph. Let the work breathe.
- **Honest.** Label NDA work, concepts, and missing metrics. Never inflate.

### Sentence patterns to use

- "The problem was not X. It was Y."
- "I bet on X because Y. We rejected Z because W."
- "The first version did X. The shipped version did Y."
- "This changed X by Y. The caveat is Z."
- "If I did it again, I would X."

### Words to avoid

Passionate, pixel-perfect, innovative, disruptive, seamless, delightful, synergy, ninja, rockstar, "wear many hats," "fast-paced environment," "worked closely with stakeholders" (show it instead).

### Example voice

> The brief was a wallet. The actual problem was trust: convincing someone whose money had always been physical to hand a balance to a screen.

> We shipped three of the four flows unchanged. The fourth failed usability twice, so we cut it. That was the right call, even though it was the most interesting screen.

|---|---|---|
| Senior Product Designer, B2B SaaS / fintech | Primary | Strategy, systems, influence, measurable impact |
| Product Designer, fintech / loyalty / CRM | Primary | End-to-end craft, research, shipped work |

| Design Systems Designer | Optional | Component architecture, adoption, documentation |
| UX Researcher | Not targeted | Research is a strength, not the whole profile |

### Level decision

**Senior Product Designer — confirmed.** The site must lead with senior evidence, not senior adjectives:

| Senior signal | Where it appears |
|---|---|
| Frame ambiguity, not just execute a brief | Project 1: one platform vs four industries → archetype model |
| Design the system, not the screen | Project 1 modular cards; Project 2 money rails; Project 3 dashboard IA |
| Influence product direction | Decision logs with options, trade-offs, and rejected paths |
| Work across functions | Agent research, PM/eng collaboration, design QA |
| Own outcomes | Outcome section with metric or honest qualitative signal |
| Raise the bar beyond one project | `/systems`: design system, FlutterFlow library, AI workflow, QA |
| Judgment about scope | What was cut, what was deliberately not built, what was reused |

If a case study cannot support the senior column, it stays at craft and weakens the claim. Fix the evidence, not the wording.

- **Visual:** warm paper, ink, one electric accent, editorial type, spatial depth.
- **Verbal:** short sentences, concrete decisions, no jargon.
- **Experiential:** 3D that explains, not impresses; motion that clarifies, not performs.

A design lead should be able to say, after one visit:
